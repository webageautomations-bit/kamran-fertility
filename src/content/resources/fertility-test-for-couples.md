## Fertility Tests for Couples: Which Tests You Need, When, and What the Results Mean

You have been trying for a while, and now someone has told you it is time to get tested. Maybe you are not sure who goes first, what the tests involve, or whether anything will hurt. Those are fair worries. **Fertility tests for couples** are more routine than most people expect, and most of them are quick, low-risk checks. This guide explains each test for both partners, when to do it, what a normal result looks like, and what Lahore labs typically charge. It also covers what the results can and cannot tell you, because one number rarely settles anything.

**Quick answer:** Most couples start with a semen analysis for him, and an ovulation check, hormone blood tests and a pelvic ultrasound for her. An HSG scan then checks whether her fallopian tubes are open. Testing begins after 12 months of trying, after six months if the woman is 35 or older, and straight away if there is a known cause.

## Who Should Get Fertility Tested, and When?

Most couples should be tested after 12 months of regular, unprotected sex without a pregnancy, and sooner when the woman is 35 or older. About 85 percent of couples conceive within a year without medical help, according to the American Society for Reproductive Medicine (ASRM). The UK's NICE guideline reports that more than 80 percent conceive within a year when the woman is under 40 (NICE CG156). So waiting is not wrong. But testing takes only a few weeks, and it answers questions that waiting cannot.

Guidelines differ slightly on age. This page follows ASRM's age bands, which are the most widely used. NICE sets its cut-off for earlier specialist referral at 36. Both agree that a known risk factor means testing should not wait.

| Situation | When to test (ASRM bands) |
| --- | --- |
| Woman under 35, no known problems | After 12 months of trying |
| Woman aged 35 to 40 | After 6 months of trying |
| Woman over 40 | Without delay |
| Irregular or missing periods, PCOS, endometriosis, past pelvic infection or pelvic surgery | Straight away |
| Man with past testicular injury or surgery, an undescended testicle, cancer treatment, or erection or ejaculation problems | Straight away |

Asking for tests does not mean something is wrong. It means you want a clear picture. If you are unsure whether you have reached that point, our short guide on when to seek a fertility consultation walks through the signs.

### When Should You Seek Specialist Advice Without Waiting?

The usual 12-month or six-month waiting periods are general guidelines for couples without known fertility risk factors. Some medical or reproductive histories justify an earlier appointment.

Consider seeking advice promptly if either partner has a known condition or history that could affect fertility. Examples include:

* Absent or very irregular periods
* Suspected endometriosis
* Previous pelvic inflammatory disease
* An ectopic pregnancy
* Pelvic or reproductive surgery
* Chemotherapy or radiation treatment
* A known testicular condition
* Significant difficulty with erections or ejaculation

This does not mean every person with one of these histories will have fertility problems. It means a clinician can decide whether testing should begin earlier rather than waiting for the usual time period.

## How Should Couples Approach Fertility Testing Together?

Fertility testing is most useful when both partners are assessed as part of the same process. It is not necessary to complete every test on the same day, but arranging the initial assessments around the same time can prevent delays and unnecessary investigations.

A practical sequence often looks like this:

1. **Attend an initial consultation together, if possible.** The doctor reviews how long you have been trying, previous pregnancies, medical history, menstrual cycles, sexual health, medicines and relevant lifestyle factors.
2. **Arrange the semen analysis early.** It is a straightforward first investigation for the male partner. If the result is abnormal, the doctor can decide whether and when it should be repeated.
3. **Review the woman's menstrual and reproductive history.** The doctor considers whether ovulation is likely to be occurring and chooses hormone tests or ultrasound based on the cycle and medical history.
4. **Check the uterus and fallopian tubes when indicated.** An HSG or another suitable investigation may be recommended depending on the woman's history and the initial findings.
5. **Review all results together.** The doctor considers the couple's age, how long they have been trying and the complete pattern of results before recommending further tests or treatment.

This is a general sequence, not a fixed package. Some couples need fewer tests, while others need additional investigations because of a particular medical history or an abnormal initial result. Not every couple needs every test listed in this article.

## What Does a Semen Analysis Test, and What Do the Results Mean?

A semen analysis checks how much semen there is, how many sperm it contains, how well they move, and what shape they have. It is usually the first test because it is simple, painless and quick. It is also the best way to avoid months of testing only one partner.

### How to Prepare

The World Health Organization (WHO) advises 2 to 7 days without ejaculation before the sample. Collect it in the container the lab gives you, and deliver it within about an hour, kept close to body temperature. Skip lubricants and saliva, which can harm sperm. Tell the lab about any recent fever, illness or new medicine. A fever can lower sperm numbers for weeks.

### What the Numbers Mean

Labs compare your result with WHO reference limits. These mark the lower end of what was seen in men whose partners conceived within a year. The current limits come from the 2021 sixth edition of the WHO laboratory manual.

| Measure | WHO 2021 lower limit | WHO 2010 lower limit |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Semen volume | 1.4 mL | 1.5 mL |
| Sperm concentration | 16 million per mL | 15 million per mL |
| Total sperm number | 39 million per sample | 39 million per sample |
| Total motility (any movement) | 42% | 40% |
| Progressive motility (swimming forward) | 30% | 32% |
| Normal shape (morphology) | 4% | 4% |
| Vitality (live sperm) | 54% | 58% |

Check which edition your lab uses. Some labs still print the older limits, which can make the same sample look slightly better or worse.

Four terms appear on many reports:

* **Oligozoospermia** means a low sperm count.
* **Asthenozoospermia** means weak movement.
* **Teratozoospermia** means a high share of abnormally shaped sperm.
* **Azoospermia** means no sperm were found in the sample.

The 4 percent shape limit surprises many men. Most sperm in a healthy man look irregular under the microscope, so a result of 5 or 8 percent on its own is not alarming. Falling below a limit does not mean a man is infertile, and sitting above it does not guarantee a pregnancy. These numbers describe odds, not verdicts.

### Why One Result Is Not the Final Word

Sperm counts swing from one sample to the next. Sperm also take about ten weeks to develop, so an illness or heat exposure can affect a sample long after it happened. That is why labs usually repeat an abnormal test after about three months. If no sperm or very few sperm are found, the repeat happens sooner.

A lot of men read an abnormal report as a judgement on themselves. It is not. It is a measurement, and several causes, such as a varicocele (enlarged veins around the testicle), a hormone imbalance or an infection, can be treated. For a plain-language walk-through, see **[what a semen analysis actually measures](https://www.kamranfertility.com/resources/male-infertility--treatment)**. If your report is abnormal, our male fertility assessment covers the next checks. You can also arrange the semen analysis itself through the clinic.

**A note about repeat testing:** The timing of a repeat semen analysis is not the same for every man. A clinician may recommend a repeat after an interval that allows for normal variation or recovery from a recent illness. If sperm are absent or extremely low, or if the result is markedly abnormal, earlier confirmation and specialist assessment may be appropriate. Follow the timing advised by the clinician rather than assuming every abnormal result must wait three months.

## Which Fertility Tests Do Women Need?

Women usually need an ovulation check, hormone blood tests, a pelvic ultrasound and a check of the fallopian tubes. AMH and HSG each get their own section below.

### Is She Ovulating?

Regular monthly periods usually mean ovulation is happening, but they do not prove it. A progesterone blood test, taken about seven days before the next period is due, shows whether an egg was released that cycle. Guidelines differ on how routine this is. NICE-style practice includes it, while ESHRE's guideline on unexplained infertility advises against routine progesterone testing in women with regular cycles (ESHRE). Your doctor decides based on your cycle pattern. If cycles are irregular, doctors also measure FSH and LH, the two pituitary hormones that drive ovulation. Our guide to the ovulation test explains how to read the results.

### Which Hormone Blood Tests Are Used?

Doctors pick from the list below based on your history. Few women need every test.

* **FSH (follicle-stimulating hormone):** tells the ovaries to grow an egg. A high level early in the cycle can point to lower reserve.
* **LH (luteinising hormone):** triggers ovulation. Raised levels are common in PCOS.
* **Estradiol:** the main oestrogen. Doctors read it alongside FSH, because a high estradiol can hide a high FSH.
* **TSH (thyroid-stimulating hormone):** thyroid problems can disturb cycles and ovulation.
* **Prolactin:** the milk hormone. High levels can stop ovulation, so it is usually checked when cycles are irregular or have stopped.
* **Testosterone:** checked when PCOS is suspected.

FSH, LH and estradiol are usually drawn on days 2 to 5 of the cycle, counting the first day of bleeding as day 1. For the full list, read our guide to the hormone test for fertility, or see **[can PCOS cause infertility](https://www.kamranfertility.com/resources/pcos-and-fertility)** if your cycles are irregular.

### What Does a Pelvic Ultrasound Show?

A pelvic ultrasound looks at the womb, the ovaries and any fluid in the pelvis. Early in the cycle it can count antral follicles, the small fluid-filled sacs that each hold an immature egg. It also finds fibroids, polyps and ovarian cysts. If you are booking a full workup for her, our female fertility assessment brings these pieces together.

## What Is a Normal AMH Level, and Does It Predict Fertility?

There is no single normal AMH level that fits every woman. AMH, or anti-Müllerian hormone, is made by the small follicles in the ovaries and gives a rough count of the egg reserve. It falls with age, and different labs use different test methods. The same blood sample can therefore give different numbers at different labs.

Labs report AMH in ng/mL or pmol/L. Multiply ng/mL by about 7 to get pmol/L. Your report should print that lab's own reference range, often split by age band. Doctors judge your number against that range, your age, your cycle history and your scan findings. Please do not compare it with a chart you found online.

AMH helps doctors plan IVF medicine doses. A level well above the lab's range for your age can also support a PCOS diagnosis. What it cannot do is tell you how long you will take to conceive naturally. It also does not measure egg quality.

**Important clarification about PCOS:** AMH may be one part of the assessment for polycystic ovary syndrome in adults, but it should not be used on its own to diagnose PCOS. A clinician considers symptoms, menstrual history, other hormone results and, where appropriate, ultrasound findings. A raised AMH result alone does not establish that someone has PCOS.

A large JAMA study followed women aged 30 to 44 with no history of infertility. It found that low AMH did not lower their chance of conceiving over the next year (Steiner et al., JAMA 2017). ESHRE's guideline likewise advises that ovarian reserve testing is not needed to predict natural conception in women with regular cycles (ESHRE). Natural conception needs one healthy egg, not a large number.

Hormonal contraception may lower the reading slightly, so tell your doctor if you take it. The test needs no fasting. If a low result has frightened you, talk it through with a doctor before drawing conclusions.

## What Is an HSG Test, and Does It Hurt?

An HSG, or hysterosalpingogram, is an X-ray test that shows the inside of the womb and whether the fallopian tubes are open. A doctor passes a thin tube through the cervix and releases a contrast dye while X-ray images are taken. It takes only a few minutes.

Most women feel period-like cramps while the dye goes in. Some feel more, some feel almost nothing. Ask your doctor beforehand whether to take a pain reliever. Light spotting and mild cramps for a day afterwards are common. Fever, heavy bleeding or severe pain are reasons to call your doctor.

The test is timed after bleeding stops and before ovulation, so there is no chance of an early pregnancy. It is not done if you might be pregnant or have an active pelvic infection. Tell the team if you are allergic to iodine or contrast dye.

**Additional safety clarification:** If you have ever had an allergic reaction to an iodinated contrast material used in an imaging procedure, tell the doctor and radiology team before the HSG. They can review the nature of the previous reaction and decide what precautions or alternative investigation may be appropriate. Do not assume that an allergy to iodine in food or on the skin is the same as a previous contrast-media reaction.

HSG matters because blocked tubes can stop IUI from working. If the tubes are shut, egg and sperm cannot meet, however good the sperm count is. NICE recommends HSG to screen for blocked tubes in women with no known pelvic disease. It suggests an ultrasound-based alternative, HyCoSy, where the expertise exists. For women with past pelvic infection, a previous ectopic pregnancy or endometriosis, NICE suggests keyhole surgery (laparoscopy) with a dye test instead. That way the surgeon can see and treat other problems at the same time (NICE CG156).

**One point about an apparent blockage:** An HSG result suggesting that a tube is blocked does not always prove that it is permanently blocked. In some cases, temporary muscle spasm near the opening of the tube or technical factors can affect the appearance. Your doctor may recommend reviewing the images or using another investigation before deciding what the finding means for treatment.

## Can You Check Fertility at Home?

Home tests can show whether you are probably ovulating and give a rough sperm concentration. They cannot check the fallopian tubes, give a full picture of egg reserve, or measure sperm shape. Here is what each one does:

| Home method | What it tells you | What it misses |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Ovulation predictor kit (urine LH test) | An LH surge, which comes roughly 24 to 36 hours before ovulation | Whether an egg was actually released, and the state of the tubes |
| Basal body temperature chart | Confirms ovulation after it has happened | The fertile window in advance |
| Cycle-tracking app | Your pattern over several months | Hormone levels; apps only estimate |
| Home sperm kit | Often only sperm concentration | Movement, shape and volume in many kits |
| Home AMH finger-prick kit | The same limited information as a lab AMH | Egg quality and natural conception chances |

Use home tracking to time sex while you wait for your appointment. Do not treat a normal home result as a reason to delay lab tests. Our ovulation test guide shows how to read kits correctly.

## How to Read Normal and Abnormal Results

Read your results as a pattern, not as single numbers. A lab report marks each value as inside or outside a reference range. That range describes most healthy people. It is not a pass mark.

* **A result inside the range** is reassuring for that one measure. It does not guarantee a pregnancy, and it does not clear the other tests.
* **A result slightly outside the range** is often repeated before anyone acts on it, because many values shift from one sample to the next.
* **A clearly abnormal result** points to a cause worth finding, such as an ovulation problem, a blocked tube or a sperm problem. It does not close the door.

Three questions help in any appointment:

1. Which of my results are outside the range?
2. Which of them change the treatment plan?
3. Which should be repeated, and when?

### What If a Fertility Test Result Is Borderline?

A borderline result sits close to the laboratory's reference limit. It is not automatically a diagnosis, and it does not necessarily mean that a couple cannot conceive.

The next step depends on which test is borderline and how it fits with the rest of the assessment. Your doctor may consider:

* **Timing:** Was the test performed on the appropriate day of the menstrual cycle or after the recommended preparation?
* **Laboratory range:** Which reference range and testing method did the laboratory use?
* **Variation:** Could the result change on repeat testing, as can happen with semen analysis and some hormone measurements?
* **Other findings:** Do the ultrasound, medical history and other test results point in the same direction?
* **Treatment relevance:** Would confirming the result change the recommended plan?

For example, an AMH result should be interpreted in the context of age, ultrasound findings and the reason the test was ordered. A semen analysis should be considered alongside its individual measures and, when appropriate, a repeat sample. A borderline result should be discussed with a clinician rather than interpreted in isolation or compared with an unrelated online chart.

## What Happens After the Tests?

Results point to one of a few paths, and a doctor ties them together rather than reading each test alone.

| Finding | Common next step |
| --- | --- |
| Ovulation problem or PCOS | Lifestyle changes, medicine to trigger ovulation, and timed intercourse; see ovulation induction and PCOS care |
| Blocked or damaged tubes | Surgery in selected cases, or IVF |
| Mild sperm abnormality | Treat the cause, such as a varicocele, infection or hormone problem; sometimes IUI |
| Severe sperm abnormality, or no sperm in semen | ICSI, where one sperm is injected into one egg; some men need surgical sperm retrieval |
| Low egg reserve or age-related decline | An earlier move to IVF, since time matters |
| All results normal ("unexplained") | See the next section |

## What If All Your Fertility Tests Are Normal?

Normal results in both partners point to unexplained infertility. It is a real diagnosis, and it is common: NICE puts it at about one in four couples (NICE CG156). It does not mean nothing is wrong, and it does not mean you cannot conceive. Standard tests check ovulation, open tubes and sperm numbers. They cannot see egg quality or how well an embryo implants.

ESHRE's 2023 guideline says the next step should rest on your prognosis, mainly the woman's age and how long you have been trying (ESHRE). For many couples, the first treatment is IUI with mild ovarian stimulation. For others, such as older women, moving to IVF sooner makes sense. Younger couples who have been trying for a shorter time may reasonably keep trying for a set period first.

Your doctor can explain these trade-offs once they see all your results together.

## Which Fertility Tests Are Not Needed for Everyone?

More tests do not always mean better care. Several heavily marketed tests are not part of a routine first round. ESHRE's 2023 guideline for couples with unexplained infertility advises against many of them as routine checks (ESHRE). A doctor may still suggest one when your history gives a specific reason.

| Test | Why it is not routine | When a doctor may suggest it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Sperm DNA fragmentation | Not advised when the semen analysis is normal; there is no standard cut-off, and the methods are not interchangeable | Specific cases, such as repeated pregnancy loss |
| Genetic testing (karyotype, gene tests) | Not advised for couples whose other results are normal | Very low or no sperm, repeated pregnancy loss, early menopause or a family history |
| Laparoscopy (keyhole surgery) | Invasive, and not advised as a routine step | Suspected endometriosis or pelvic disease, or a worrying HSG |
| Immune, clotting and uterine natural killer cell tests | Not advised routinely; evidence that treating abnormal results helps is weak | A known autoimmune or clotting condition |
| Endometrial biopsy (womb lining sample) | Strongly advised against without another reason | A specific suspected womb-lining problem |
| AMH as a pregnancy predictor | Does not predict natural conception | IVF planning, egg freezing, or irregular cycles |

A good habit is to ask your doctor why each test is being ordered and what you will do differently with the answer.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What is the first test done for infertility?

A semen analysis is usually the first test, because it is simple, painless and gives answers quickly. For the woman, the first steps are a history, an ovulation check and hormone blood tests. Starting with both partners saves time.

### Do both partners need fertility tests?

Yes. Fertility depends on both partners, and a problem on one side can hide a problem on the other. About one in four couples end up with no clear cause after testing.

### On which day of the cycle should fertility tests be done?

It depends on the test. FSH, LH and estradiol are usually taken on days 2 to 5, counting the first day of bleeding as day 1. Progesterone is checked about seven days before the next period. AMH can usually be tested on any day, and an HSG is done after bleeding stops and before ovulation.

### How long do fertility test results take?

Reporting ranges from the same day to a few days. Many hormone tests and AMH results come back within one to two days, and a pelvic ultrasound report is usually given on the day. HSG reports commonly follow within a day or two. Ask the lab for its reporting time when you book.

### Does a fertility test hurt?

Blood tests and ultrasounds cause little discomfort, and a semen sample needs no needles. The HSG is the one test many women find uncomfortable, with period-like cramps for a few minutes.

### How many times should a semen analysis be repeated?

If the first result is abnormal, labs usually repeat it after about three months. If no sperm or very few sperm are found, the repeat happens sooner. A normal first result usually does not need repeating.

### Can I get tested if my periods are regular?

Yes. Regular periods suggest ovulation, but they say nothing about the tubes or the sperm. Testing makes sense after 12 months of trying, or after six months if you are 35 or older.

### Is IUI 100% successful?

No. No fertility treatment is guaranteed, and IUI works only in some cycles. The chance per cycle depends mainly on the woman's age, the cause of infertility and sperm quality. Your doctor can give a realistic estimate once the tests are complete.

### Is IUI halal in Islam?

Most scholars and fiqh councils permit IUI when it uses the husband's sperm and the wife's egg within a valid marriage. Donor sperm and donor eggs are not accepted. If you want certainty about your own situation, ask a scholar you trust.

### How much do three rounds of IUI cost?

The total depends on the medicines, the monitoring scans and the clinic, so one fixed figure would mislead. Multiply the per-cycle cost by three, then add any extra medicine or scan costs. Our IUI cost guide lists what a cycle usually includes.

### Can I conceive with IUI?

Yes, many couples do, especially when the tubes are open and the sperm count is only mildly reduced. IUI suits couples less well when the tubes are blocked or sperm quality is severely reduced. That is why testing comes first.

### Can fertility tests tell me if I can have a baby?

No test can promise that. Tests find problems that can be treated, and they show which problems waiting will not fix. They describe your chances. They do not set them in stone.
